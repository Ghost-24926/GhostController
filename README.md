Maksos arrived at the robotics room ten minutes early, the way he always did, because the early minutes were the only ones that belonged to him. The door was still propped open with a brick the janitor had left there in September and never taken back. Inside, the fluorescent lights hummed over long tables scarred by solder and screwdriver tips. Bins of gears sat in neat rows along the back wall. A half-built chassis waited on the middle table, its wheels off, its battery pack taped down with blue painter’s tape that had gone gray at the edges.

He set his backpack on the chair nearest the window and unzipped the front pocket. The notebook was there, the one with the graph-paper pages and the coffee stain on the cover that looked, if you tilted it, like a small continent. He opened it to the page he had worked on the night before: a sketch of a gripper that used a single servo and a rubber band instead of the two-servo claw everyone else was copying from the tutorial. The idea was simple. The rubber band held the fingers closed. The servo only had to open them. Less weight, less current, fewer wires to snag on the course tape. He had written the torque calculation in the margin and circled the number twice.

The hallway outside filled with voices. Maksos closed the notebook halfway and kept his hand on it.

They came in as a group because they always came in as a group. There were four of them who mattered and a few others who drifted at the edges, laughing when they were supposed to laugh. The one who walked first was taller than the rest and wore the team hoodie with the sleeves pushed to his elbows. His name was Gleb. Behind him came Jonah, who carried the good multimeter like it was a badge, and two boys whose names Maksos knew and did not like to say out loud because saying them made the room smaller. They dropped their bags. One of the bags hit the leg of Maksos’s chair.

“You’re in my seat,” Gleb said.

Maksos looked at the chair. It was the same chair he had used since the first week. The cushion had a split in it that he had learned to avoid with his left thigh. “I was here first,” he said.

Gleb smiled the way people smile when they have already decided the next sentence. “Move.”

Maksos moved. He took the notebook and the backpack and went to the end of the table, the end nearest the parts bins, where the light was worse and the power strips were full. He opened the notebook again. The sketch was still there. The circled number was still there. For a moment that was enough.

Robotics met three times a week after the last bell, in a room that had once been a home-economics lab. The ovens were gone. The counters remained, too high for comfortable soldering, so the school had brought in the long tables and left the counters as shelves. On those shelves lived the things the club owned and the things the club had broken: spare Arduino boards in antistatic bags, a bin of wheels with one size missing, a 3D printer that jammed if you looked at it wrong, a cardboard box labeled MOTORS in marker that had bled. The sponsor, Mr. Ellison, taught physics in the morning and arrived to robotics ten minutes late with a mug and a stack of papers he never quite put down. He believed in students figuring things out. He said this often. He stood at the front and talked about the regional competition in March, about autonomous points, about the importance of a clean wiring harness. Then he sat at his desk and graded, and the room organized itself the way rooms do when an adult is present but not watching.

Maksos had joined because the flyer on the science-wing bulletin board showed a small robot crossing a line of black tape, and because the caption under the photo said anyone could build one. He had read that sentence twice. Anyone. He had gone home and told his mother, who worked the evening shift at the clinic and came home with the smell of antiseptic still in her sleeves. She had said yes without asking what it cost. The cost, it turned out, was a twenty-dollar lab fee and a pair of safety glasses, which he already owned from chemistry. He had paid the fee out of the envelope he kept in his desk, the one labeled SCHOOL in his own handwriting, and he had walked into the room on the first Tuesday of September with the glasses in his pocket and the feeling that he was about to become a person who built things that moved.

The first weeks were almost what the flyer had promised. They learned to strip wire. They learned why a loose ground makes a motor twitch like a nervous animal. Maksos’s hands were steady. He had spent years assembling model kits at the kitchen table while his mother slept through the afternoon, and the patience of that work transferred. When Mr. Ellison demonstrated a solder joint, Maksos watched the iron, not the teacher’s face. He went home and practiced on scrap perfboard until the joints shone. He labeled his wires. He wrote down what he tried and what failed. The notebook filled.

The trouble started small, the way weather starts. A comment about his backpack, which was an old one with a broken zipper he had fixed with a safety pin. A laugh when he mispronounced a part name on the first day and then pronounced it correctly forever after, as if the first mistake were the only version that counted. Someone moved his safety glasses. Someone ate the granola bar he had set beside his notebook and, when he looked up, said they thought it was communal. He stopped bringing food. He started arriving early. He started leaving his notebook in his lap instead of on the table.

By October the comments had a shape. Gleb’s group had claimed the middle of the room and the best boards and the working printer. They called their robot The Beast, which was a name with no engineering content and a great deal of volume. Maksos worked on a smaller platform he had named, only in the notebook, Line. Line was meant to do one thing well: follow the tape, correct quickly, and not fall apart when the course had a sharp corner. He did not announce this. Announcing things had begun to feel like putting them on a table where other people could reach them.

“What’s that, a toy?” Jonah said one afternoon, leaning over Maksos’s chassis. Jonah’s breath smelled like the orange soda from the vending machine by the gym.

“It’s a line follower,” Maksos said.

“We’re doing a line follower too. Ours is going to be faster.”

“Okay.”

“Mr. Ellison said the speed bonus is, like, half the score.”

“I know.”

Jonah flicked the rubber band on the gripper prototype Maksos had clamped to the table. The band snapped back. “This is janky.”

“It’s a test.”

“It’s janky,” Jonah said again, louder, so the others could hear. They laughed. The laugh was not about the rubber band. Maksos had understood that for a while. The rubber band was only the nearest object.

He waited until they turned away, then he wrote in the notebook: rubber band slips if the servo horn is plastic; try metal horn or double band. He underlined double band. His ears were hot. His handwriting stayed even.

The beating, when people later asked him to name it, was not one afternoon. It was a series of afternoons that learned how to be worse. The first time hands were involved, it was in the hallway outside the room, after Mr. Ellison had already left to move his car before the parking lot closed. Gleb bumped Maksos into the lockers with his shoulder. The impact was dull and complete. Maksos’s notebook bent against the metal. A gear he had been carrying in his pocket—a small brass one he had found in the scrap bin and meant to measure—fell and rolled under the lockers and was gone.

“Watch it,” Gleb said.

Maksos watched the place where the gear had disappeared. He did not pick a fight he would lose in a hallway with no adults. He had a younger sister who needed him to come home. He had a mother who did not need a phone call from the school. He straightened the notebook against his leg until the crease was only a crease and not a tear. At home he sat on his bed and opened to the gripper page and found that the circled torque number had cracked down the middle where the notebook had folded. He copied the page onto a fresh sheet. Copying it made his hands stop shaking.

He told no one. Telling required a sentence he did not yet know how to say. If he said they hit me, an adult would ask who and when and whether he had hit back, and the answers would turn into a meeting, and the meeting would turn into Gleb’s version, which would be cleaner because Gleb’s version had friends in it. Maksos had the notebook. He had Line, which was starting to roll in a straight line on the classroom floor when he tested it during the early minutes. He decided the work was the thing he could protect, and he protected it by pretending the rest was weather.

Weather accumulated. In November they took the battery out of his robot while he was in the bathroom and hid it in the bin of broken wheels. He found it because he looked in every bin, methodically, the way his mother looked for a pulse: not panicked, not finished until the last place. The battery was warm from being held. Someone had been carrying it in a pocket. He put it back in the chassis and checked the voltage and did not look at their faces, because looking would be a kind of asking, and asking was what they wanted.

They called him Maksos like it was a joke they had invented, stretching the second syllable, though it was simply his name, the name his father had chosen before his father moved to another city and became a voice on a phone that rang less and less often. “Maks-ohhhss,” Jonah would say, passing behind his chair. “Did your robot die again, Maks-ohhhss?” The robot had not died. A header pin had bent. Maksos straightened it with pliers and reseated the cable and Line woke up. He did not offer this information. Information was a handle they could grab.

Mr. Ellison noticed the quiet, once. He stopped at the end of the table and looked at the chassis. “Clean layout,” he said. “You’re keeping the weight low.”

“Yes, sir.”

“How’s the team treating you? You finding a group?”

The question was kind and badly timed. Gleb was ten feet away, holding a screwdriver, watching without appearing to watch. Maksos felt the room tilt toward whatever he said next.

“I’m good on my own for now,” he said. “I want to understand the whole robot before I join a subsystem.”

Mr. Ellison nodded, relieved by a sentence that sounded like initiative. “That’s a solid approach. Don’t isolate too long, though. Robotics is a team sport.”

“I know.”

After Mr. Ellison moved on, Gleb said, softly enough that it would not carry to the desk, “Team sport. You hear that, solo boy?”

Maksos heard it. He also heard the motor on Line spin up in his head, the way he heard it when he could not sleep: a thin electric whine, then the correction, left sensor, right sensor, the robot nudging itself back to the tape. He held onto that sound. It was more precise than the room.

The day they broke the gripper was a Thursday. Rain had turned the parking lot to a dark mirror, and people came into the building shaking water off their sleeves. Maksos had finished the double-band version. It opened and closed on the bench with a small, satisfying motion, like a mouth deciding to speak and then deciding not to. He had weighed it. He had written the new mass in the notebook. He had allowed himself, for the length of one breath, to think that at regionals a referee might watch Line pick up the bonus piece and set it on the platform and that the points would be real points, not theoretical ones.

He went to the sink at the back of the room to wash solder flux off his fingers. Soap, water, the brown smell of the lab. When he turned around, Gleb was standing at his table with the gripper in one hand. Jonah had the notebook.

“Give that back,” Maksos said.

“We’re just looking,” Gleb said. He flexed the fingers. The rubber bands stretched. “See, this is what I mean. You think you’re smart, but this would never hold the piece. The piece is heavier than your little test cube.”

“The piece is a hundred and twenty grams. I calculated it.”

“You calculated it,” Jonah said, in the stretched voice. He flipped pages. “Look at all the little numbers. Little Maksos numbers.”

Maksos stepped forward. Gleb stepped to meet him. The gripper was between them, absurd and small. Gleb’s free hand came up and shoved Maksos in the chest, not a tap, a real shove that sent him into the counter. The counter edge caught him across the ribs. Pain flashed white and then settled into a hard line. His elbow knocked a bin of resistors. They spilled, a bright rain of color bands across the tile.

“Pick those up,” Gleb said.

“Give me my stuff.”

Jonah tore a page out of the notebook. The sound was small and final. He crumpled it and tossed it at Maksos’s feet. It was the gripper page, the one with the torque number copied clean after the hallway. Maksos saw the circle, saw the crease, saw the page become trash on a floor covered in resistors.

Something in him moved faster than the strategy. He lunged for the notebook. Jonah yanked it back. Gleb swung the gripper, not like a weapon exactly, more like a person demonstrating contempt, and the printed fingers caught Maksos across the cheek. The plastic split. A rubber band snapped and stung his wrist. He tasted blood where his teeth had cut the inside of his lip. The room, which had been pretending not to see, went quiet in the particular way of people who are seeing everything and choosing a side by not moving.

“Hey,” someone said, a girl from the edge group, uncertain.

Mr. Ellison’s chair scraped. He had been in the supply closet. He came out with a box of filament and stopped. “What is going on?”

Gleb let the broken gripper drop on the table. “He flipped out,” he said. “We were looking at his design and he went crazy. He knocked the resistors over.”

Maksos stood with his hand on his cheek. The notebook was still in Jonah’s hands. Blood was a penny taste. He thought of his sister doing homework at the kitchen table. He thought of his mother clocking in. He thought that if he cried, the story would harden around him in a shape he could never sand off.

“He tore my notebook,” Maksos said. His voice came out thinner than he wanted. “He hit me with the gripper.”

“I didn’t hit you. You ran into it.”

Mr. Ellison looked at the resistors, the crumpled page, the split plastic, the mark on Maksos’s face that was already red. He looked tired. He looked like a man who had wanted the afternoon to be about filament. “All of you, back up,” he said. “Gleb, Jonah, office. Maksos, nurse. Now.”

The nurse gave him a cold pack and a form. The form asked what happened in a box that was too small. He wrote: Another student struck me in the face with a robot part during club. He tore a page from my design notebook. He did not write about the months before. The months did not fit in the box.

His mother came from the clinic in her scrubs, rain on her shoulders, eyes already at the place on his cheek. She did not make a scene. She put her hand on the back of his neck, the way she did when he was sick, and she listened to the assistant principal use words like incident and conflicting accounts and two-day suspension for the boys who had been named. She asked whether the suspension included the competition season. The assistant principal said the club sponsor would decide participation. Maksos watched a crack in the floor tile and understood that decide was a word with a trapdoor in it.

At home his sister, nine and precise, examined the cold pack as if it were a specimen. “Did you hit them back?” she asked.

“No.”

“Why?”

“Because I wanted to stay.”

She considered this with the seriousness she brought to math worksheets. “That’s a bad reason if they’re going to keep doing it.”

“I know.”

His mother sat at the table after his sister was in bed. The scrubs were in the laundry. She had made tea she was not drinking. “You can quit the club,” she said. “You don’t have to earn the right to be left alone.”

Maksos turned the mug. “If I quit, they get the room.”

“The room is not a prize.”

“Line is almost working.”

She was quiet for a while. The refrigerator hummed. Rain ticked the window over the sink. “Then you tell me the rest,” she said. “Not the one bad day. The rest. And tomorrow we talk to Ellison together, and we write it down, all of it, dates if you have them. You don’t protect people who are hurting you by being easy to ignore.”

He almost said he had been protecting the work, not them. The distinction collapsed under her look. He got the notebook. The torn page was taped in, wrinkled, still legible. He started at September. He surprised himself with how much he remembered: the granola bar, the gear under the locker, the battery in the wheel bin, the stretched pronunciation of his name, the shove, the comments about his backpack, the way the group went quiet when a teacher passed and loud when the closet door closed. His mother wrote on a legal pad. She did not interrupt except to ask him to repeat a date. When he was done, the pad was full and his tea was cold and the mark on his cheek had become a bruise the color of a storm cloud.

“This is enough,” she said. “This is more than enough.”

Mr. Ellison read the pages in his physics classroom before school, with Maksos and his mother in the student desks, which made all three of them look slightly wrong. The fluorescent lights were the same model as the ones in the robotics room. Maksos watched the teacher’s face and waited for the relief, the minimizing, the suggestion that boys roughhouse. It did not come. Mr. Ellison took off his glasses and pinched the bridge of his nose.

“I should have seen it,” he said.

“Yes,” Maksos’s mother said. She did not soften it.

“I’ll remove them from the club through regionals. I’ll document this with the office. And I’ll be in the room, not in the closet.” He looked at Maksos. “Your robot. The gripper. I’d like to see the calculation when you’re ready. Not as a favor. Because if the mass is right, the mechanism is interesting.”

Maksos nodded. He did not trust his voice yet. Interesting was a small word. He held it anyway.

The weeks after were not a movie. Gleb and Jonah were gone from the room, and their friends did not apologize; they simply occupied less space and laughed less often. Some of the laughter moved to the cafeteria, where Maksos ate with his notebook open so he would have somewhere to look. A boy from the edge of the group muttered a word at him near the water fountain. Maksos wrote the word down later, not because he needed it, but because his mother had told him that records were a kind of light. He gave the note to Mr. Ellison. The mutterer stopped muttering. Consequences, Maksos learned, were not lightning. They were a slow change in what people believed they could get away with.

He rebuilt the gripper. The split finger became a lesson in layer orientation on the printer. He ran the double rubber band through a groove so it could not walk off the horn. He tested the grip with a block he had weighted to one hundred and twenty grams on a kitchen scale while his sister counted down from ten like a mission controller. The fingers held. He wrote it in the notebook in ink.

Line learned the course in pieces. First the straight. Then the gentle curve. Then the corner that had defeated the first algorithm, where the sensor array lost the tape and the robot spun in a lost circle until Maksos rewrote the recovery to reverse one wheel and creep. He stayed after Mr. Ellison stayed. Sometimes they did not talk. The teacher graded at the desk with the door open and the hallway visible, and Maksos soldered, and the room sounded like a room where work was the loudest thing.

At regionals, in a gym that smelled like floor wax and popcorn, Line ran the autonomous route in thirty-one seconds and placed the piece on the platform. The piece was the official one, not the kitchen-scale block, and for a half second Maksos thought the fingers would slip. They did not. The referee’s flag went up. A volunteer at the scoring table typed something. Points appeared on a monitor. They were ordinary points. They were also the exact number he had circled, in another form, months ago, before a hallway and a torn page and a bruise.

His mother and sister were in the stands in their ordinary coats. His sister waved both hands. His mother nodded once, the nod she used when a thing was true and did not need decoration. Gleb was not there. The absence was not victory. Victory was the robot completing the corner without the lost spin, the wiring harness staying put, the notebook in his bag with every failed attempt still in it, because the failures were part of the machine as much as the brass inserts and the code.

Afterward, in the pit area, a judge leaned over the chassis and asked about the gripper. Maksos explained the single servo, the rubber bands, the mass budget, the groove that kept the band from walking. He used his ordinary voice. The judge asked to see the notebook. Maksos hesitated only a moment, then opened it. The taped page showed. The judge did not comment on the tape. He commented on the torque margin.

“You left yourself some room,” the judge said.

“Yes,” Maksos said. “I needed room.”

On the bus home he slept with his forehead against the window and woke when the town lights started. His ribs, which had hurt for two weeks after the counter, did not hurt. His cheek was only a memory of color. He thought about the boy he had been in September, standing under the flyer that said anyone could build one, believing the sentence meant the room would be fair because the work was fair. The work was fair. The room had not been. Both facts fit in him now without one erasing the other.

He did not become a person who liked what had happened. He did not owe anyone a story about how the cruelty improved him. The cruelty had wasted hours and torn a page and put blood in his mouth and taught his sister a lesson she should not have had to study. What he kept was narrower and more his: the habit of writing things down, the early minutes, the recovery move on the sharp corner, the knowledge that leaving would have been allowed and staying had been a choice made with his eyes open. He kept his name without the stretched syllable. He kept Line, scuffed, functional, heavier by one brass insert than the first sketch and lighter than anything that needed two servos to do a job one servo could do.

In the spring the club got a new student, a seventh grader with a backpack newer than Maksos’s and hands that shook when the soldering iron came on. Maksos saw the shake. He also saw two older students notice the shake and glance at each other with a look he recognized, the look that measures whether a person is alone. He put his iron in the stand. He walked to the end of the table where the light was worse and the power strips were full, the end that had been his because someone stronger had decided the middle was not for him.

“You can sit here,” he said. “The ground pin on that cable is loose. I’ll show you.”

The seventh grader sat. Mr. Ellison was at the front of the room with the door open, watching the tables, present in the way he had promised. Outside, the day was ordinary. Inside, a motor spun up, thin and electric, and corrected itself, and Maksos put his hand on the notebook so it would not be taken, and then, because no one was reaching for it, he took his hand away and let the page lie open where the work could be seen.
